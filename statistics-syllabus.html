Module 1 — Introduction to Statistics, Data & Presentation

1. Meaning and Definition

Statistics is the systematic process of collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing and interpreting data. In a singular sense it refers to statistical methods; in a plural sense it can refer to numerical facts.

Statistical investigation cycle: identify problem → collect data → classify/tabulate → present → analyze → interpret → communicate conclusion.

2. Scope and Uses

Statistics is used in business, economics, science, medicine, education, government and research. It helps summarize large data sets, compare groups, measure uncertainty, estimate population characteristics and test claims.

3. Population, Sample, Parameter and Statistic

Population is the complete set under study. A sample is a subset selected from it. A numerical characteristic of a population is a parameter; the corresponding numerical characteristic computed from a sample is a statistic.

4. Types of Data

Qualitative
Categories such as branch or blood group.
Quantitative
Numerical measurements or counts.
Discrete
Countable values such as number of students.
Continuous
Measurements such as height and weight.
Primary
Collected first-hand for the investigation.
Secondary
Previously collected data from reports, books or databases.

5. Collection Methods

Common methods include direct personal investigation, interview, questionnaire, observation and experiment. A good questionnaire uses clear, unbiased questions and a suitable sampling plan.

6. Classification and Frequency Distribution

Raw data may be grouped into classes. For each class determine lower/upper limits or boundaries, class width, frequency and cumulative frequency. Class mark is the midpoint.

Class mark = (Lower limit + Upper limit)/2

7. Diagrammatic and Graphic Presentation

Bar diagram: categorical/discrete comparisons. Pie chart: parts of a whole. Histogram: continuous grouped frequency data using adjacent rectangles. Frequency polygon: joins class-mark frequencies. Ogive: cumulative-frequency curve used to locate median and quartiles.

Example: If a class has 40 students and 12 scored between 70–80, its relative frequency is 12/40 = 0.30 or 30%.

Module 2 — Measures of Central Tendency

Central tendency gives a representative value. The major measures are arithmetic mean, geometric mean, harmonic mean, median and mode.

1. Arithmetic Mean

Individual: x̄ = Σx/n
Discrete/grouped: x̄ = Σfx/Σf
Weighted: x̄w = Σwx/Σw

Mean uses all observations and is mathematically convenient, but extreme values can pull it strongly.

Step-by-step: Data = 5, 7, 8, 10, 10. Step 1: Σx=40. Step 2: n=5. Step 3: x̄=40/5=8.

2. Assumed Mean and Step-Deviation

x̄ = A + Σfd/Σf, where d=x−A
For equal class width: x̄ = A + h(Σfu/Σf)

These methods simplify calculations when values are large or grouped.

3. Geometric Mean

GM = ⁿ√(x₁x₂...xₙ)
log GM = Σlog(x)/n

GM is useful for compound growth, ratios and multiplicative changes. For ordinary real-valued use, observations are positive.

4. Harmonic Mean

HM = n / Σ(1/x)

HM is useful for rates such as speed when equal distances are involved. For positive observations, HM ≤ GM ≤ AM.

5. Median

For individual data, arrange values. Odd n: position (n+1)/2. Even n: average the two central observations.

Grouped median = l + [(N/2 − cf)/f]h

l = lower boundary of median class; N = total frequency; cf = cumulative frequency before median class; f = median-class frequency; h = class width.

Example: For 3, 5, 7, 9, 20, the median is 7. It is less affected by the extreme 20 than the mean.

6. Mode

Mode is the most frequent value. For grouped continuous data, identify the modal class and use:

Mode = l + [(f₁−f₀)/(2f₁−f₀−f₂)]h

Here f₁ is modal-class frequency, f₀ preceding frequency and f₂ succeeding frequency.

7. Empirical Relation

Mode ≈ 3(Median) − 2(Mean)

This approximate relation is useful for moderately skewed distributions, not as a universal identity.

MeasureStrengthLimitation
MeanUses all valuesSensitive to outliers
MedianRobust and suitable for skewed dataLess algebraically convenient
ModeMost common valueMay be non-unique or unstable
Practice: Find mean, median and mode for 4, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. Explain which measure is preferable when one observation is extremely large.

Module 3 — Measures of Dispersion

1. Meaning

Dispersion measures variability around a central value. Two data sets can have the same mean but very different spreads, so a measure of central tendency should often be accompanied by dispersion.

2. Range

Range = L−S
Coefficient = (L−S)/(L+S)

Simple and quick, but based only on the largest and smallest observations.

3. Quartile Deviation

QD=(Q₃−Q₁)/2
Coefficient=(Q₃−Q₁)/(Q₃+Q₁)

It describes the spread of the central half of the data and is less affected by extremes.

4. Mean Deviation

MD = Σ|x−A|/n
Grouped: MD = Σf|x−A|/Σf

Use absolute deviations so positive and negative differences do not cancel.

Method: find A (mean/median) → compute each |x−A| → multiply by f if grouped → add → divide by n or Σf.

5. Variance and Standard Deviation

Population variance σ² = Σ(x−μ)²/N
Population SD σ = √σ²
For frequency data: σ²=Σf(x−x̄)²/Σf
Shortcut: σ²=Σx²/N−μ²
Solved: Data 2,4,6. Mean = 4. Deviations = −2,0,2. Squares = 4,0,4. Variance = 8/3 = 2.667. SD = √2.667 ≈ 1.633.

6. Coefficient of Variation

CV=(σ/x̄)×100

CV is a relative measure. Lower CV generally means greater consistency when comparing series with meaningful positive means.

7. Covariance

Cov(X,Y)=Σ[(x−x̄)(y−ȳ)]/n

Its sign indicates direction of joint movement, but its magnitude depends on measurement units. Correlation standardizes this relationship.

Practice: For 5,7,8,10,10 find range, mean deviation about mean, variance, SD and CV. Show the deviation and squared-deviation columns.

Module 4 — Probability, Distributions & R

1. Basic Probability

P(A)=favourable outcomes/total equally likely outcomes
P(A′)=1−P(A)

Probability lies from 0 to 1. Events can be mutually exclusive, exhaustive, independent or dependent.

2. Addition and Multiplication Rules

P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)
P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B|A)
If independent: P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)

3. Conditional Probability and Bayes' Idea

P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B)

Bayes' theorem reverses conditional probabilities and is useful when evidence is observed and the underlying cause must be assessed.

4. Binomial Distribution

Requirements: fixed number n of independent trials, two outcomes per trial, constant success probability p and q=1−p.

P(X=x)=ⁿCₓpˣqⁿ⁻ˣ
Mean=np
Variance=npq
SD=√npq
Example: Exactly 2 heads in 3 fair tosses = ³C₂(1/2)²(1/2) = 3/8.

5. Poisson Distribution

P(X=x)=e⁻λ λˣ/x!
Mean=λ, Variance=λ

It models counts in a fixed interval under an appropriate constant-rate independent-event assumption.

6. Normal Distribution

Normal distribution is continuous, bell-shaped and symmetric about μ. Mean=median=mode. Standardization is:

Z=(X−μ)/σ

Use a standard-normal table or software to obtain areas/probabilities.

7. R Basics

x <- c(2,4,6,8) mean(x) sd(x) # Binomial pbinom(2, size=5, prob=0.4) # Poisson dpois(2, lambda=3)
Practice: (1) Binomial n=5,p=.4: find P(X=2). (2) Poisson λ=3: find P(X=2). (3) Normal μ=60, σ=5: find Z for X=70.

Module 5 — Correlation, Hypothesis Testing, t-Test, ANOVA & Regression

1. Correlation

Correlation measures linear association. Pearson's r ranges from −1 to +1. Sign indicates direction; magnitude indicates strength for a linear relationship.

r=Cov(X,Y)/(σxσy)

Correlation does not by itself establish causation.

2. Hypothesis Testing Procedure

  1. State H₀ and H₁.
  2. Choose significance level α.
  3. Select appropriate test and calculate statistic.
  4. Find p-value or compare with critical value.
  5. Reject or fail to reject H₀.
  6. Write the conclusion in the context of the problem.
Interpretation: A p-value is the probability, under the null model, of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed. It is not the probability that H₀ is true.

3. Type I and Type II Errors

Type I: reject a true H₀. Its probability is controlled by α. Type II: fail to reject a false H₀. Its probability is β; power is 1−β.

4. t-Test

When population variance is unknown, t-based procedures are commonly used for means. For a one-sample test:

t=(x̄−μ₀)/(s/√n), df=n−1

Other forms include independent two-sample and paired t-tests. Select the form based on the study design.

5. ANOVA

ANOVA compares several means by separating variability into between-group and within-group components.

F=MS_between/MS_within

For one-way ANOVA, degrees of freedom are k−1 between groups and N−k within groups. A significant F suggests at least one group mean differs; post-hoc comparisons may then locate differences.

6. Simple Linear Regression

ŷ=a+bx
b=Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ)/Σ(x−x̄)²
a=ȳ−bx̄

The slope b is the expected change in predicted y for one unit increase in x. Residual = observed y − predicted y.

7. Multiple and Generalized Linear Regression

Multiple regression uses multiple predictors: y=β₀+β₁x₁+...+ε. Generalized linear models use an appropriate response distribution and link function; logistic regression is common for binary outcomes.

8. Numerical Answer Format

Always write Given → Formula → Substitution → Calculation → Answer → Interpretation. Keep intermediate values to enough precision and round only at the final stage unless the question specifies otherwise.

🔥 Important Exam Questions & Numerical Checklist

Short theory: population vs sample, primary vs secondary data, discrete vs continuous, mean/median/mode, variance vs SD, covariance vs correlation.
Graph questions: frequency table, histogram, frequency polygon, ogive, pie/bar diagram.
Numericals: AM/GM/HM, grouped median/mode, MD, QD, variance, SD, CV.
Probability: conditional probability, Bayes, binomial, Poisson and normal-standardization.
Inference: hypotheses, p-value, Type I/II errors, t-tests and ANOVA.
Regression: covariance, Pearson r, regression line, slope/intercept and interpretation.
Exam rule: Do not skip the equation-solving part. For every numerical, show the formula first and substitute the values line by line.
