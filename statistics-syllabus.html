Module 1 — Introduction to Statistics

Meaning and Definition

Statistics is the systematic science of collecting, classifying, presenting, analyzing and interpreting numerical information. The objective is not merely to calculate numbers but to obtain meaningful conclusions for decision-making.

Scope

The subject covers data collection, classification, tabulation, diagrams and graphs, measures of central tendency, dispersion, probability, probability distributions, correlation, regression and inferential tests.

Types of Data

  • Qualitative: non-numerical categories such as gender or department.
  • Quantitative: numerical observations such as marks or income.
  • Discrete: countable values, e.g. number of students.
  • Continuous: measurements, e.g. height or weight.
  • Primary: collected first-hand for the current study.
  • Secondary: already collected and obtained from reports, books, databases, etc.

Collection and Presentation

Questionnaires, interviews, observation and experiments are common collection methods. After collection, data may be classified into frequency tables and presented using bar charts, pie charts, histograms, frequency polygons, frequency curves and ogives.

For a grouped frequency table, calculate class width, class boundaries, class mark (midpoint) and cumulative frequency where required.

Example: Marks 10, 12, 12, 15, 18 can be classified into suitable intervals and represented with a frequency distribution. Always state the variable, units and sample size.

Module 2 — Measures of Central Tendency

Central tendency represents a data set with one typical value. The syllabus includes arithmetic mean, geometric mean, harmonic mean, median and mode, along with their merits, demerits and numericals.

Arithmetic Mean

x̄ = Σx/n
Frequency: x̄ = Σfx/Σf
Weighted: x̄w = Σwx/Σw

Mean uses every observation and is suitable for algebraic analysis. Its weakness is sensitivity to very large or very small values.

Solved: 5, 7, 8, 10, 10. Σx=40 and n=5. Therefore x̄=40/5=8.

Geometric Mean

GM = ⁿ√(x₁x₂...xₙ)
log GM = Σlog(x)/n

GM is appropriate for growth rates, ratios and compound changes. Values should generally be positive for the ordinary real-valued formula.

Harmonic Mean

HM = n / Σ(1/x)

HM is especially useful for rates. For positive observations, HM ≤ GM ≤ AM.

Median

Arrange data. For odd n, the median is the (n+1)/2th item; for even n, average the two central observations.

Grouped median = l + [(N/2 − cf)/f]h

Here l is lower boundary of median class, N total frequency, cf cumulative frequency before it, f its frequency and h class width.

Mode

Mode is the most frequently occurring value. It is useful when the most common size/category is important.

Mode = l + [(f₁−f₀)/(2f₁−f₀−f₂)]h

Comparison

MeasureBest featureMain limitation
MeanUses all observationsAffected by extremes
MedianRobust for skewed dataLess suitable for algebraic work
ModeMost common valueMay have multiple/no clear mode
Practice: Find AM, GM, HM, median and mode for 4, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. Also state which measure is most suitable if an extreme outlier is present.

Module 3 — Measures of Dispersion

Dispersion describes how widely observations are scattered around a central value. Low dispersion means values are more consistent.

Range

Range = L−S
Coefficient of Range = (L−S)/(L+S)

It is simple but depends only on two extreme observations.

Mean Deviation

MD = Σ|x−A|/n
Frequency: MD = Σf|x−A|/Σf

A can be mean or median. Absolute deviations prevent positive and negative deviations from cancelling.

Quartile Deviation

QD = (Q₃−Q₁)/2
Coefficient QD = (Q₃−Q₁)/(Q₃+Q₁)

QD measures the spread of the central 50% of observations.

Variance and Standard Deviation

σ² = Σ(x−x̄)²/n
σ = √σ²
Shortcut: σ² = Σx²/n − x̄²
Numerical: For 2,4,6: x̄=4. Deviations are −2,0,2; squares are 4,0,4. Variance=8/3=2.667 and SD=√2.667≈1.633.

Coefficient of Variation

CV = (σ/x̄)×100

CV compares relative variability. The series with smaller CV is generally considered more consistent.

Covariance

Cov(X,Y)=Σ[(x−x̄)(y−ȳ)]/n

Positive covariance indicates variables tend to move in the same direction; negative indicates opposite movement. Covariance depends on measurement units.

Practice numerical: For 5,7,8,10,10 calculate range, mean deviation about mean, variance, SD and CV. Show every substitution step.

Module 4 — Probability & Probability Distributions

Basic Probability

P(A)=favourable outcomes/total equally likely outcomes
0≤P(A)≤1

Complement: P(A′)=1−P(A). Addition rule: P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B). For mutually exclusive events, P(A∩B)=0.

Conditional Probability & Independence

P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B)
If independent: P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)

Conditional probability is used when information about one event changes the probability of another.

Binomial Distribution

Conditions: fixed n trials, two outcomes per trial, independent trials and constant success probability p. Let q=1−p.

P(X=x)=ⁿCₓ pˣqⁿ⁻ˣ
Mean=np
Variance=npq
Example: Exactly 2 heads in 3 fair tosses = ³C₂(1/2)²(1/2)=3/8.

Poisson Distribution

P(X=x)=e⁻λ λˣ/x!
Mean=λ, Variance=λ

It models event counts in a fixed interval when the average rate is λ and events occur independently.

Normal Distribution

The normal distribution is continuous, symmetric and bell-shaped. Mean, median and mode coincide. Total area under the curve is 1.

Z=(X−μ)/σ

Standardization converts a normal variable to the standard normal scale for probability calculations.

R for Probability

R provides functions such as dbinom()/pbinom() for binomial and dpois()/ppois() for Poisson calculations. Distribution plots can be produced using R's plotting functions.

Practice: (1) Binomial: n=5,p=.4, find P(X=2). (2) Poisson: λ=3, find P(X=2). (3) Normal: μ=60, σ=5, find Z for X=70.

Module 5 — Correlation, Covariance, Tests & Regression

Correlation

Correlation measures the direction and strength of association between variables. Pearson's coefficient r lies between −1 and +1.

r = Cov(X,Y)/(σxσy)

Positive r means variables tend to rise together; negative r means one tends to fall as the other rises. Correlation measures association, not causation.

Hypothesis Testing

State null hypothesis H₀ and alternative H₁, choose significance level α, compute a test statistic, obtain a p-value/critical comparison and make a conclusion in context. A small p-value provides evidence against H₀.

t-Test

A t-test is commonly used for means when population variance is unknown. A one-sample statistic is:

t=(x̄−μ₀)/(s/√n), df=n−1

Depending on the problem, independent-samples or paired-samples t-tests may be used.

ANOVA

Analysis of Variance compares several group means by partitioning total variation into between-group and within-group variation.

F = MS_between / MS_within

A sufficiently large F statistic can provide evidence that not all group means are equal.

Simple Linear Regression

ŷ=a+bx
b=Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ)/Σ(x−x̄)²
a=ȳ−bx̄

The slope b represents expected change in y for a one-unit change in x in the fitted linear model.

Multiple & Generalized Linear Regression

Multiple regression uses several predictors: y=β₀+β₁x₁+β₂x₂+…+ε. Generalized linear models allow non-normal response distributions and use a link function, such as logistic regression for binary outcomes.

How to Write a Numerical

  1. Write the given data.
  2. Write the required formula.
  3. Substitute values clearly.
  4. Show intermediate calculations.
  5. Give the final answer with units/interpretation.
