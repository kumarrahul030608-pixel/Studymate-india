Module I — Introduction to Statistics

Definition & Meaning

Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing and interpreting data. It helps convert large amounts of observations into useful summaries for decision-making.

Scope

Statistics is used in business, economics, science, engineering, medicine, education and research. Descriptive statistics summarizes observed data, while inferential statistics uses samples to draw conclusions about populations.

Types of Data

  • Qualitative: non-numerical characteristics such as branch or gender.
  • Quantitative: numerical data; discrete values are countable and continuous values are measurable.
  • Primary: collected first-hand for a specific study.
  • Secondary: obtained from existing records, reports or databases.

Methods of Data Collection

Common methods include observation, interviews, questionnaires, schedules, experiments and use of published records.

Presentation of Data

Data can be presented using tables, bar diagrams, pie charts, histograms, frequency polygons, frequency curves and ogives. Graphical presentation makes patterns and comparisons easier to see.

Frequency Distribution

A frequency distribution groups observations into values or class intervals and records how often each occurs. Important terms include class limits, class boundaries, class width, class mark and cumulative frequency.

Module II — Measures of Central Tendency

Arithmetic Mean

Mean is obtained by dividing the sum of observations by the number of observations. It uses every observation but can be affected by extreme values.

Individual: x̄ = Σx / n
Frequency: x̄ = Σfx / Σf

Geometric Mean

GM is useful for ratios, growth rates and multiplicative changes. For n positive observations, GM is the nth root of their product.

GM = (x₁x₂...xₙ)^(1/n)

Harmonic Mean

HM is useful when averaging rates such as speed over equal distances.

HM = n / Σ(1/x)

Median

Median is the middle value after arranging observations. For grouped data, locate the median class using N/2 cumulative frequency.

Grouped median = L + [(N/2 − cf)/f] × h

Mode

Mode is the most frequently occurring value. For grouped data, identify the modal class with highest frequency.

Mode = L + [(f₁−f₀)/(2f₁−f₀−f₂)] × h

Merits & Demerits

Mean is algebraically convenient but sensitive to outliers. Median is robust and suitable for skewed data but is less suitable for algebraic manipulation. Mode is easy to understand and useful for the most common category but may be non-unique.

Solved Numerical — Mean

Data: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50. Sum = 150 and n = 5.

Mean = 150/5 = 30

Module III — Measures of Dispersion

Need for Dispersion

Two data sets can have the same mean but very different spread. Dispersion measures how far observations vary around a central value.

Range

Range = Maximum − Minimum
Coefficient of Range = (Max−Min)/(Max+Min)

Mean Deviation

Mean deviation is the average of absolute deviations from a chosen central value.

MD about A = Σ|x−A| / n

Quartile Deviation

QD = (Q₃−Q₁)/2
Coefficient QD = (Q₃−Q₁)/(Q₃+Q₁)

Variance & Standard Deviation

Variance is the average squared deviation. Standard deviation is its positive square root and is expressed in the same units as the observations.

Population variance σ² = Σ(x−x̄)²/n
σ = √σ²

Coefficient of Variation

CV compares relative variability between series with different means.

CV = (SD / Mean) × 100

Numerical — Standard Deviation

For 2, 4, 6, 8: mean = 5. Deviations are −3, −1, 1, 3; squared deviations sum to 20.

Variance = 20/4 = 5
SD = √5 ≈ 2.236

Module IV — Probability & Probability Distributions

Basic Probability

Probability measures the likelihood of an event. For equally likely outcomes, P(A) = favorable outcomes / total outcomes.

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1
P(A') = 1 − P(A)

Addition & Multiplication Rules

P(A∪B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)
If independent: P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)

Normal Distribution

A continuous, symmetric bell-shaped distribution described by mean μ and standard deviation σ. Mean, median and mode coincide in a perfectly normal distribution.

Binomial Distribution

Used for a fixed number n of independent trials with two outcomes per trial and constant success probability p.

P(X=x) = C(n,x)pˣ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ˣ
Mean = np; Variance = npq

Poisson Distribution

Useful for counts of events occurring in a fixed interval when events occur independently at a constant average rate.

P(X=x)=e⁻λ λˣ/x!
Mean = Variance = λ

R for Probability

R can calculate probabilities and plot distributions using functions such as dbinom(), pbinom(), dpois(), ppois() and dnorm().

Module V — Correlation, Tests & Regression

Correlation

Correlation measures the strength and direction of linear association between two variables. Pearson's correlation coefficient ranges from −1 to +1.

r = Cov(X,Y)/(σx σy)

Covariance

Covariance indicates whether two variables tend to increase together or move in opposite directions. Unlike correlation, its magnitude depends on measurement units.

T-Test

A t-test is used to compare means when assumptions such as approximate normality are appropriate and population variance is unknown. Common forms include one-sample, independent two-sample and paired t-tests.

ANOVA

Analysis of Variance tests whether three or more population means differ. It compares between-group variation with within-group variation using an F statistic.

Simple Linear Regression

Regression models a dependent variable Y using an explanatory variable X. The fitted line is Y = a + bX, where b is the slope.

Multiple & Generalized Linear Regression

Multiple regression uses two or more predictors. Generalized linear models extend regression to response distributions beyond normal errors and use a link function to connect predictors to the expected response.
